The morning sheet

Four locations on one page, plus the half a point of sale cannot see. The top is built from POS data. The bottom is built from supplier invoices: PDFs and phone photos read into line items, priced per kilogram, and compared against what the same item cost six to sixteen weeks ago. Together they answer the question a dashboard never does, which is whether yesterday actually made any money.

How the sheet reads. Every line is derived, not typed. A site is flagged when it moved against its own normal for this weekday, not against the group, because comparing a ninety-seat room to a food-hall counter tells you nothing. Labour is flagged on its share of sales. Voids are flagged as a rate, so three voids in a four-hundred-order day stay quiet and three in a forty-order day do not.
Where the model is, and where it is not. A model reads each invoice once, when it arrives, and its only job is transcription: supplier, date, description, pack, quantity, line total, exactly as printed. It is never asked to calculate. Every unit price, every percentage and every dollar on this page is ordinary arithmetic over those line items, which is why the lines are added up and checked against the invoice's own stated total before anything is believed. It also means an open page like this one costs nothing to look at: nothing here calls a model when you load it.
