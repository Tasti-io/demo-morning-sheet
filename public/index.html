The morning sheet

Four locations on one page, plus the half a point of sale cannot see. The top is built from POS data. The bottom is built from supplier invoices: PDFs and phone photos read into line items, priced per kilogram, and compared against what the same item cost six to sixteen weeks ago. Together they answer the question a dashboard never does, which is whether yesterday actually made any money. The Tomorrow tab turns the other way: it forecasts tomorrow, proposes the rota that fits it, and waits for a manager to approve or change it.

The problem this is about

A point of sale tells you what you sold. It does not know what you paid, because that arrives separately, as PDFs from distributors and paper in a folder. So the one question an owner actually has in the morning, whether yesterday made any money, is the one question no POS dashboard answers.

Most multi-location operators fill that gap by hand for a while. Someone opens three portals, copies figures into a spreadsheet, and eyes it. Somewhere around the third location that stops happening, and after it stops, a supplier price can drift for eight months before anybody notices.

What the top half does

It pulls yesterday from the point of sale and puts every location on one line. The only judgement it makes is which sites are worth your attention, and it makes that judgement against each site's own normal for that weekday, never against the group. A ninety seat room and a food hall counter have nothing to say about each other. A Sunday has nothing to say about a Tuesday.

Labour is flagged on its share of sales. Voids are flagged as a rate, so three voids in a four hundred order day stay quiet and three in a forty order day do not.

What the bottom half does

Supplier invoices are forwarded to one address. Each is read once into line items: supplier, date, description, pack size, quantity, line total. Pack sizes are normalised, so one distributor's "5 x 2.5 KG" and another's "CASE 25 LB" become the same price per kilogram and can finally be compared.

From there it watches for three things. A price that has moved against what the same item cost six to sixteen weeks ago. The same item bought from two suppliers at two prices in the same month. And an ingredient whose cost has risen while the dish it goes into has not changed price since February.

Where the AI is, and where it deliberately is not

A model reads each invoice once, when it arrives, and its only job is transcription: what the page says, as printed. It is never asked to calculate. Every unit price, every percentage and every dollar figure here is ordinary arithmetic over those line items.

That boundary is not decoration. It means the product lines can be added up and checked against the invoice's own goods subtotal before anything is believed, so a misread digit surfaces as "this does not reconcile" instead of as a confident sentence about your money. It also means this page calls no model when you open it, so a link like this one costs nothing to look at, however many people do.

What it refuses to do

Three kinds of line are held back rather than absorbed: a product no catalog entry covers, a pack size that cannot be read, and an invoice whose lines do not add up to its own subtotal. They are listed for a person instead of being guessed at. A price history that quietly swallowed them would be wrong in a way nobody could see, which is worse than one with gaps you can point at.

Nothing is reported unless it is worth about $150 a month or more. A 35% rise on an item you spend $15 a month on is arithmetically true and operationally useless, and a page that prints it is a page that stops being read by the second week.

What the Tomorrow tab does

The sheet and the invoices look back. The Tomorrow tab looks forward. For each room, half hour by half hour, it forecasts tomorrow's orders, sets them against what the usual rota for that weekday can carry, and proposes the smallest set of changes that moves people toward the hours that need them. A manager approves each change or keeps the usual shift.

Where the forecast comes from

The starting point is plain: for every half hour, the median of the last four ordinary same weekdays. Statutory holidays are left out, so Labour Day does not teach it what a normal Monday looks like. On top of that come adjustments for the BC calendar, the long weekends and the holidays themselves, and for the weather.

The weather is real. It comes from Open-Meteo, for Vancouver, for the day you are looking at. What the weather does to each room is an assumption, because Harbour & Co is fictional, and every assumption is labelled where it is used. On your own POS history those effects are measured instead: how much a wet Friday actually moved each of your rooms last year. This page never quotes an accuracy figure, because on invented history there is nothing honest to measure one against.

Why it starts from your rota

A planner that built a rota from nothing would be easier to write and harder to trust, because every line would need checking. This one starts from the rota a manager would copy forward anyway, so only the differences need reading, and each comes with a named person and a reason in plain words. What it assumes about productivity is learned from that same rota. So it never says "you need 3.4 servers". It says your own Friday puts six servers on at 14:00, and the rush starts at 17:30.

The rules it will not break

These are British Columbia's, from the Employment Standards Act, and they are the part a planner built for the United States does not know.

No shift past 8 paid hours (s.40), because the time over costs 1.5 times. Where the usual rota already does that, it says so with the cost, and leaves the fix to the manager. A meal break after 5 hours (s.32), treated as unpaid on the assumption the person is free to leave. At least 2 paid hours for anyone called in (s.34); in practice it never adds a shift shorter than three. On a statutory holiday, 1.5 times plus an average day's pay (s.46), so it wants a much bigger rush before it adds anyone, and the people who are off are still owed an average day's pay (s.45), which it shows separately because no rota can change it. Managers sit outside the overtime rules (Employment Standards Regulation s.34(f)).

It also refuses two things on its own account. One shift per person per day, across the whole group. And it never invents a person: if a rota calls for more people than a room employs, or a rush needs someone who is already working, it says so instead of filling the line with a name that does not exist.

Approve, or keep the usual shift

Each change can be approved or overridden, with a reason. The reason matters more than the click: in a live build it is stored with the change, so a pattern of overrides becomes a correction to the forecast rather than a running argument with it. Approving here sends nothing. In a live build the approved rota goes to the scheduling tool already in use, 7shifts or Square Team, and the people whose shifts moved get a message.

Like the rest of this page, the Tomorrow tab calls no model. The forecast and the rota are arithmetic you can check, and opening the page costs nothing however many people do.

On your own numbers

The fastest way to see whether any of this is real is to test the hard part on something only you have.
