The problem this is about

A point of sale tells you what you sold. It does not know what you paid, because that arrives separately, as PDFs from distributors and paper in a folder. So the one question an owner actually has in the morning, whether yesterday made any money, is the one question no POS dashboard answers.

Most multi-location operators fill that gap by hand for a while. Someone opens three portals, copies figures into a spreadsheet, and eyes it. Somewhere around the third location that stops happening, and after it stops, a supplier price can drift for eight months before anybody notices.

What the top half does

It pulls yesterday from the point of sale and puts every location on one line. The only judgement it makes is which sites are worth your attention, and it makes that judgement against each site's own normal for that weekday, never against the group. A ninety seat room and a food hall counter have nothing to say about each other. A Sunday has nothing to say about a Tuesday.

Labour is flagged on its share of sales. Voids are flagged as a rate, so three voids in a four hundred order day stay quiet and three in a forty order day do not.

What the bottom half does

Supplier invoices are forwarded to one address. Each is read once into line items: supplier, date, description, pack size, quantity, line total. Pack sizes are normalised, so one distributor's "5 x 2.5 KG" and another's "CASE 25 LB" become the same price per kilogram and can finally be compared.

From there it watches for three things. A price that has moved against what the same item cost six to sixteen weeks ago. The same item bought from two suppliers at two prices in the same month. And an ingredient whose cost has risen while the dish it goes into has not changed price since February.

Where the AI is, and where it deliberately is not

A model reads each invoice once, when it arrives, and its only job is transcription: what the page says, as printed. It is never asked to calculate. Every unit price, every percentage and every dollar figure here is ordinary arithmetic over those line items.

That boundary is not decoration. It means the product lines can be added up and checked against the invoice's own goods subtotal before anything is believed, so a misread digit surfaces as "this does not reconcile" instead of as a confident sentence about your money. It also means this page calls no model when you open it, so a link like this one costs nothing to look at, however many people do.

What it refuses to do

Three kinds of line are held back rather than absorbed: a product no catalog entry covers, a pack size that cannot be read, and an invoice whose lines do not add up to its own subtotal. They are listed for a person instead of being guessed at. A price history that quietly swallowed them would be wrong in a way nobody could see, which is worse than one with gaps you can point at.

Nothing is reported unless it is worth about $150 a month or more. A 35% rise on an item you spend $15 a month on is arithmetically true and operationally useless, and a page that prints it is a page that stops being read by the second week.

On your own numbers

The fastest way to see whether any of this is real is to test the hard part on something only you have. Forward one supplier invoice to the address below, PDF or a photo of the page, and you will get back your own line items with a price per kilogram beside each, plus whatever did not reconcile. No account, no connection to your POS, no call first.
